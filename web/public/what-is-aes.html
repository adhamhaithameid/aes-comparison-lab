    What is AES?

    A beginner-friendly introduction — no prior crypto knowledge needed.

    The short version

    AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) is a recipe for scrambling data so only someone with the right key can read it. It is the most widely used symmetric encryption algorithm in the world — used in HTTPS, Wi-Fi, disk encryption, and VPNs.

    An analogy: a locked box

    Imagine a box with a lock that has one key. You and your friend both have a copy of that key. You put a message in the box, lock it, and send it. Anyone who intercepts the box sees only metal — they cannot read the message. Your friend unlocks it with their copy. That is symmetric encryption: one shared key for both locking and unlocking.

    What AES actually does

    • Takes a fixed-size chunk of data called a block (128 bits = 16 bytes).
    • Mixes it with a key (128, 192, or 256 bits) through several rounds of substitution, shifting, and mixing.
    • Produces a 128-bit block of ciphertext that looks random.

    AES-128 uses 10 rounds; AES-192 uses 12; AES-256 uses 14. More rounds = more security, slightly slower.

    Why "standard"?

    In 2001 NIST published AES as FIPS 197 after a public competition. The winner was Rijndael, designed by Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen. Being a standard means everyone implements the same algorithm, so systems can interoperate.

    What AES does NOT do

    • It does not prove who sent the message (no authentication on its own).
    • It does not detect tampering (use GCM or a MAC for that).
    • It does not manage or distribute keys (you must handle keys securely).
